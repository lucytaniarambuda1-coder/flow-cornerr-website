How to Start a Small Business in South Africa

The full step-by-step guide: from idea to first customer, for every kind of business.

← Back to Start a Business

By Lucy Mukhethwa · Career Strategist · Last reviewed October 2026
Good to know

This guide is general information to help you get started. It is not legal, tax or financial advice, and rules, fees and funding change. Last reviewed: October 2026. Always confirm the details on the official page linked in each section, and speak to an accountant or attorney for anything that affects your money or liability. Applying to government agencies is free: never pay anyone to "fast-track" a grant or registration.

Starting a business is one of the most exciting things you can do with your skills, and one of the easiest to get tangled up in. Between the paperwork, the tax rules and the money, many people never start. This guide walks you through the whole path, in order, for the kinds of businesses most South Africans actually start, with real links to the official places where each step happens.

If you are not sure whether you want a business or a job, that is a fair place to be. Start with the free career assessment, and come back when you know.

1. Are you ready? A quick self-check

You do not need to feel ready. You need to be honest about where you are. Answer these for yourself, on paper:

  1. What problem am I solving, and for whom? If you cannot name the person, you are not ready to price or promote it.
  2. Do people already pay for this? Someone paying a competitor is good news: it means the market exists.
  3. What skills do I already have, and what am I missing? List both. Missing skills can be learned, hired or partnered for.
  4. How much money do I need to start, and how long can I last without income? Work out a number, even a rough one.
  5. How many hours a week can I really give it? Many successful businesses start part-time alongside a job.
  6. Who will support me, and who will challenge me? You need both.

2. Find and test your idea

Before spending money, test whether anyone wants what you are offering.

  • Talk to ten possible customers. Ask what they do now, what annoys them, and what they pay. Do not pitch. Listen.
  • Pre-sell. Offer your product or service to a few people before it fully exists: a WhatsApp message with a price and a date is enough. Real orders beat compliments.
  • Start small. One product, one service, one area. You can widen it once it works.
  • Check the competition. Note who they are, what they charge and what customers complain about. That complaint is your opening.

3. How it works for different types of business

The legal steps in sections 4 to 7 apply to every business, but what you need to start differs a lot by type. Find yours below. Many rules (especially permits and health rules) are set by your local municipality, so always ask them before you spend money on premises or equipment.

Service business or freelancing

Examples: consulting, cleaning, tutoring, photography, design, bookkeeping, writing, event planning.

  • Start-up needs: usually the lowest of any type: skills, a phone, data, and perhaps a laptop.
  • First steps: define one clear offer and a price; build a small portfolio or three case studies (offer your first jobs at a discount in exchange for a testimonial); set up a simple invoice and a one-page contract.
  • Watch out for: underpricing, working without a deposit, and mixing client money with your own. Most service businesses start as a sole proprietor and register a company later.

Retail, spaza and township trade

Examples: spaza shops, tuck shops, general dealers, clothing and accessories, airtime and data resellers.

  • Start-up needs: stock, premises or a stall, a way to track stock, and often a refrigerator or equipment.
  • Rules to check first: spaza shops and other food-selling outlets are required to register with their municipality. Municipalities typically ask for a business licence, zoning approval, a food-safety certificate and an ID, and some also ask for company and tax registration. Requirements differ by municipality, so contact your local business-licensing and environmental health offices. See the government's guide to registering spaza shops.
  • First steps: pick a location with foot traffic and permission to trade; buy only the fast-moving stock you can sell in a week; keep a daily cash book; learn your margins before you set prices.
  • Watch out for: over-stocking, extending credit you cannot collect, and selling expired or unsafe food.

Food and catering

Examples: home baking, catering, food stalls, a food truck, a small restaurant.

  • Start-up needs: equipment, ingredients, packaging, transport, and a hygienic place to prepare food.
  • Rules to check first: a food business generally needs a Certificate of Acceptability from your municipality's environmental health department before you trade. An inspector checks your premises against the national food-hygiene regulation (R638). It is issued for specific premises and is not transferable. This usually applies to a home kitchen, a stall and a delivery vehicle as well as a restaurant. A business licence is a separate municipal permit.
  • First steps: contact your municipality's environmental health office; get the application form; prepare your premises to the standard they will inspect; start with a small, simple menu.
  • Watch out for: trading before you have the certificate (fines or closure are possible), and pricing without counting your own time.

Online business and e-commerce

Examples: online shops, reselling, digital products, social-media services, courses, apps.

  • Start-up needs: a product or service, a way to take payment, delivery or a way to deliver digitally, and an online presence (social page, WhatsApp Business, or a simple website).
  • Rules to check first: if you collect customers' personal information (names, phone numbers, addresses) you must handle it responsibly under the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA). The Consumer Protection Act also covers returns, honest advertising and clear prices.
  • First steps: test with a handful of orders through WhatsApp or Instagram before you build a store; work out delivery cost per order; set a clear returns policy.
  • Watch out for: paying for stock you have not tested, hidden delivery costs, and chasing followers instead of buyers.

Farming and agriculture

Examples: vegetables, poultry, livestock, urban farming, agro-processing.

  • Start-up needs: land or access to it, water, inputs (seed, feed), equipment, and a buyer for what you produce.
  • First steps: secure a buyer or market before you plant or buy animals; start on a small area to learn the costs; ask your provincial Department of Agriculture about extension officers and training; ask the funders in our funding guide about agricultural support.
  • Watch out for: weather and disease risk, long delays before income, and selling at low prices because you have no storage or transport.

Trades and construction

Examples: electrical, plumbing, building, painting, welding, carpentry, security.

  • Start-up needs: tools, transport, insurance, and proof you can do the work.
  • Rules to check first: some trades are regulated. For example, electrical and plumbing work often requires a registered person and a compliance certificate, and construction businesses bidding for public-sector work register with the Construction Industry Development Board (CIDB). Check what applies to your trade before you quote.
  • First steps: get your trade qualification and any required registration; start with small jobs for referrals; get a written quote and deposit for every job.
  • Watch out for: working without the right registration, unpaid invoices, and quoting without counting materials and transport.

Buying a franchise

A franchise gives you a proven brand and system in return for fees and rules.

  • Know your rights: under the Consumer Protection Act regulations, a franchisor must give you a signed disclosure document at least 14 days before you sign, and you have a 10-business-day cooling-off period after signing. Confirm the current wording with a franchise attorney before you commit.
  • First steps: talk to current franchisees (not only the ones the franchisor picks); work out the total cost including fees and royalties; check whether the franchisor belongs to the Franchise Association of South Africa (FASA).
  • Watch out for: promised profits you cannot verify, and agreements you do not fully understand.

A side hustle growing into a business

If you already sell on the side, you are ahead of most people. Keep your job while you track your sales for three months, separate the money, and register once you have steady customers or a client who asks for a tax invoice.

4. Write a simple business plan

A business plan does not need to be long. The government's small-business guidance says it should state what you will do, how, when, and why you expect to succeed, backed by research. One to three pages is enough to start. Cover:

  • What you sell and who buys it.
  • Why you: your skills and experience.
  • Your costs and prices: start-up costs, monthly running costs, price per sale, and how many sales you need to break even.
  • A 12-month cash-flow estimate: money in and money out, month by month.
  • How you will find customers.
  • How much funding you need, and what for.

Seda helps entrepreneurs write business plans free of charge, and the Department of Employment and Labour publishes a practical guide to starting and running your own business (PDF) with templates. You can also read the government's own page on starting your own business.

5. Choose a legal structure

This decides how you are taxed, who is responsible if things go wrong, and how funders see you.

The old "close corporation" (CC) is no longer registered for new businesses, so a (Pty) Ltd is the usual choice once you outgrow a sole proprietorship. Many people start as a sole proprietor and register a company once the business is earning steadily.

6. Register your business and tax

Register a company (if you chose a (Pty) Ltd)

  1. Create an account on BizPortal. You sign in with your South African ID.
  2. Optionally reserve a name. You can register without one and use the registration number as your name until you choose.
  3. Enter your director details. BizPortal checks each director's ID against Home Affairs, so spell names exactly as on the ID. A company needs at least one director.
  4. Pay the fee. At the time of writing BizPortal lists R125 without a name and R175 with a name; confirm the current fee on the site. BizPortal's own step-by-step guide shows each screen.
  5. Keep your documents. Once registered, you must file annual returns with CIPC.

Register for tax with SARS

  • Companies: registering a company with CIPC automatically creates an income-tax reference number with SARS. You then register on eFiling to submit returns.
  • Sole proprietors: register yourself with SARS as a taxpayer; you may need to pay provisional tax during the year.
  • Turnover Tax: an optional, simpler tax for micro businesses. SARS's Budget 2026 guidance says the turnover limit rises from R1 million to R2.3 million from 1 April 2026. Confirm the current limit on the SARS Turnover Tax page.
  • VAT: compulsory once your taxable sales pass the threshold, and optional below it. The 2026 Budget announced a rise in the compulsory threshold to R2.3 million and the voluntary threshold to R120,000, but SARS's small-business VAT page still shows R1 million and R50,000, so check the current figures on the SARS VAT page before you decide.
  • More from SARS: the small businesses section and its Turnover Tax requirements.

If you will employ people

Register as an employer: UIF and the Compensation Fund (COIDA) can both be registered through BizPortal if you have employees, and PAYE and the Skills Development Levy apply once your payroll reaches the thresholds. Ask an accountant or Seda adviser which apply to you, and what a contract must contain.

Local and industry permits

Your municipality may require a business licence, zoning approval for your premises, or health and fire approvals. Ask the business-licensing office before you sign a lease. If you want to supply government, register on the Central Supplier Database (it is free).

7. Set up your money properly

  • Open a separate business bank account. Banks offer starter accounts for small businesses. Never run the business through your personal account.
  • Record every rand. A simple spreadsheet or a free bookkeeping app is enough at the start: date, what, who, in, out.
  • Price properly. Price = your costs + your time + a profit. Do not copy a competitor's price without knowing their costs.
  • Pay yourself a small, regular amount and leave the rest in the business until you are stable.
  • Keep a tax pot. Move a percentage of every sale into a separate savings account for tax.
  • Get an accountant for the first year-end, even if you do the rest yourself.

8. Get your first customers

  • Tell everyone you know, clearly, what you do and who it is for. First customers are usually people who already trust you.
  • Use WhatsApp Business and a single social page instead of five half-finished ones. Post proof: results, photos, testimonials.
  • Claim your Google Business Profile if customers search for a local service.
  • Ask for referrals after every job, and say thank you.
  • A simple website helps once you have something to show. It does not need to be big or expensive.
  • Be consistent. Post, follow up and ask every week, not only when it is quiet.

9. Funding, in one paragraph

Most small businesses start with savings, a small loan from family, or a first customer's deposit, and then apply for outside funding once they have a registered business, a plan and some sales. For young people there are real options, including the NYDA grant, sefa loans, and several private programmes. Each has its own requirements. Read our full funding guide for youth and entrepreneurs, and be careful with anyone who asks you to pay a fee to "unlock" a grant.

10. Common mistakes

  • Spending on a logo, website and stock before testing demand.
  • Mixing personal and business money.
  • Pricing too low and trying to make it up in volume.
  • Skipping registrations and permits, then losing a contract or paying a fine.
  • Doing everything alone. A mentor, an adviser or a partner saves expensive mistakes.
  • Giving up in month three. Most businesses take longer than expected to find their footing.

11. Your first 90 days checklist

Days 1 to 30: prove it
  • Talk to ten possible customers
  • Pre-sell to at least three
  • Write your one-page business plan and cash flow
  • Decide your structure and price
Days 31 to 60: set it up
  • Register (CIPC and SARS) or register as a taxpayer if a sole proprietor
  • Open the business bank account
  • Check municipal permits for your type of business
  • Set up records and a simple invoice
Days 61 to 90: sell it
  • Serve your first customers and collect testimonials
  • Ask each customer for a referral
  • Review your numbers: what sold, what cost, what profit
  • Book a free session with Seda or NYDA, and check funding options

12. Where to get free help

  • Seda (Small Enterprise Development Agency): free advice, business plans, training and mentoring through its branches. Seda is being merged with sefa into a new agency, so check the Department of Small Business Development site for current branch details: dsbd.gov.za.
  • NYDA: business management training and grants for ages 18 to 35, through the NYDA website, its branches, or the call centre on 0800 58 58 58.
  • Local municipality: business licensing, zoning and environmental health.
  • Business Partners and sefa: small-business finance and mentorship. See the funding guide.
