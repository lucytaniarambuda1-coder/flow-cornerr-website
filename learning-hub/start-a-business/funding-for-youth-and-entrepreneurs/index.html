Funding for Youth & Entrepreneurs

Grants, loans and free support: who qualifies, how to apply, and how to avoid scams.

← Back to Start a Business

By Lucy Mukhethwa · Career Strategist · Last reviewed October 2026
Good to know

This guide is general information to help you get started. It is not legal, tax or financial advice, and rules, fees and funding change. Last reviewed: October 2026. Always confirm the details on the official page linked in each section, and speak to an accountant or attorney for anything that affects your money or liability. Applying to government agencies is free: never pay anyone to "fast-track" a grant or registration.

Funding is one of the biggest questions for anyone starting a business. This guide explains the main kinds of funding, then lists the main programmes open to South African youth and entrepreneurs, what each is for, and where to apply. We do not quote amounts that different sources disagree on, because caps and deadlines change. Check the official page for the current figures.

Grants, loans, equity and savings: what is the difference?

  • Grant: money you do not pay back, usually for a specific use, with conditions and reporting.
  • Loan: money you repay, with interest. Funders look hard at whether the business can repay.
  • Equity: an investor gets a share of your business. Less common for very small businesses.
  • Your own money: savings, a family loan or a customer's deposit. Often where the first rand comes from, and funders like to see you have put something in.
Avoid funding scams

Applying to government funders is free. Never pay anyone who promises you a grant, "guaranteed" approval or a "fast track". Apply only through the funder's official website, branch or call centre, and be careful with sites that only repeat funding information, because their amounts and dates are often out of date.

How to get funding-ready

Most funders look for the same things. Work through this list before you apply:

  1. A registered business. Some funders accept sole proprietors, but many want a registered entity. See how to register.
  2. Tax compliance. A registered SARS profile and up-to-date returns.
  3. A clear business plan with a cash-flow forecast and what you will spend the money on.
  4. Quotations from suppliers for anything you plan to buy (often three).
  5. Bank statements from a business account, and certified copies of your ID.
  6. A practised pitch. Ten minutes: who you serve, what you sell, how it makes money, what you need.
  7. Evidence of sales or demand: orders, invoices, letters of intent, testimonials.

If you are applying to the NYDA

  1. Register on the NYDA portal or visit a branch; the call centre is 0800 58 58 58.
  2. Complete the NYDA business management training. See the government's page on business management training for entrepreneurs.
  3. Prepare your documents and application form (see the step-by-step guide on Vuk'uzenzele).
  4. Give your pitch and go through the due-diligence assessment.
  5. Apply well before your 35th birthday: the NYDA sets a minimum lead time, and sources quote different periods, so confirm it with them.

Before you apply: your checklist

  • I read the official page for this programme, not a summary on another site.
  • I meet the age, ownership and turnover rules.
  • I know whether applications are open, and the closing date.
  • My documents are certified and up to date.
  • I am not paying anyone to apply for me.
  • I have a plan for what the money will do and how I will show results.
